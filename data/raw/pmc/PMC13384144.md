# Prevalence, trends and inequalities of childhood undernutrition according to a Composite Index of Anthropometric Failure (CIAF): a systematic review and meta-analysis

PMCID: PMC13384144 | PMID: 42468995 | Journal: BMJ global health (2026)

## Abstract

AbstractBackgroundThe Composite Index of Anthropometric Failure (CIAF) and its extensions-the Composite Index of Severe Anthropometric Failure (CISAF) and extended CIAF (eCIAF)—capture overlapping forms of child undernutrition but remain under-reported at global and regional levels. We aimed to estimate the prevalence of CIAF, CISAF and eCIAF among children under 5 years and assess inequalities by sex, residence, household wealth and maternal education.MethodsWe systematically searched major databases, including Embase, MEDLINE (PubMed), Scopus, CINAHL, ProQuest, Global Index Medicus and the Cochrane Library, without language or geographic restrictions. Random-effects meta-analyses estimated pooled prevalence. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q and I² statistics. Subgroup analyses, meta-regression, sensitivity analyses and publication bias assessments were conducted. Inequalities were quantified using prevalence ratios (PRs), prevalence differences, the Concentration Index and the Slope Index of Inequality (SII).ResultsWe included 107 studies from 18 countries, predominantly low- and middle-income countries in South Asia and Africa (notably India, n=54), comprising over 2.6 million under-5 children for CIAF, 590 744 for eCIAF and 29 905 for CISAF. The pooled prevalence of CIAF was 49.07% (95% CI 46.00% to 52.14%), declining from 59.8% in 2005 to 45.9% in 2024. The pooled prevalence of CISAF and eCIAF was 12.77% and 40.41%, respectively. CIAF prevalence did not differ significantly by sex (pooled PR: 1.04, 95% CI 0.99 to 1.09) but was higher in rural than in urban settings (pooled PR: 1.18, 95% CI 1.10 to 1.27). Substantial socioeconomic inequalities were observed, with higher prevalence among children from the poorest households (pooled PR: 1.71, 95% CI 1.42 to 2.05) and among those whose mothers had no formal education (pooled PR: 1.55, 95% CI 1.26 to 1.90). Absolute inequalities were further confirmed by SII estimates for wealth (−31.28%) and maternal education (−26.60%).ConclusionsCIAF prevalence remains alarmingly high in low-income settings, disproportionately affecting children in rural areas, poorer households and those born to mothers with limited education.

## Background

Childhood undernutrition remains a significant public health issue, with stunting, wasting and underweight commonly used as standard nutritional indicators. Stunting in children is an indicator for chronic undernutrition, which manifests in linear growth failure over time, wasting signals acute undernutrition with loss of lean and fat tissue often due to illness or lack of food. On the other hand, underweight can indicate both chronic and acute forms of undernutrition.

Globally, nearly half of all child mortality is linked to malnutrition. In 2020, an estimated 149 million children under 5 (22%) were stunted and 45 million (7%) experienced wasting. According to the WHO, much of the burden of childhood undernutrition is concentrated in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). In many LMICs, not only does undernutrition remain prevalent, but with multiple anthropometric failures where children simultaneously experience more than one form of undernutrition. This increasing trend of co-occurrence of multiple forms of undernutrition further complicates child health outcomes due to profound impairment in physical and cognitive development, increased vulnerability to illness and raised mortality risk. Affected children by the combination of stunting, wasting and/or underweight face compounded health risks.

Conventional measures of undernutrition such as stunting, wasting and underweight each capture different aspects of a child’s nutritional status. However, these indicators are not mutually exclusive as a child may experience more than one form of undernutrition at the same time. Relying on these indicators separately can underestimate the true burden of undernutrition in a population. These indices often fail to capture the full extent of undernutrition, particularly when multiple forms of anthropometric failure coexist in children. To address this limitation, the Composite Index of Anthropometric Failure (CIAF) was introduced by Svedberg, which aggregates all three forms of undernutrition into a single index, providing a more comprehensive measure, capturing the overall extent of undernutrition and identifying children who are not affected by any form of anthropometric failure. The CIAF combines categories such as wasting only, wasting and underweight, stunting and underweight, stunting and wasting, stunting only and underweight only, offering a better means of identifying more children affected by single or combined forms of undernutrition.

Although several primary studies have reported CIAF in various settings, their findings are often inconsistent, geographically limited and methodologically diverse, limiting our understanding of the burden and disparities in multiple forms of undernutrition among children. Existing systematic reviews have primarily focused on either stunting, wasting or underweight using traditional indicators; however, no review to date has comprehensively assessed the burden of undernutrition using the CIAF among children under 5. Furthermore, no review has examined inequalities in CIAF across different inequality metrics such as gender, age, socioeconomic status, geographic location or maternal education. This gap underscores the need for a comprehensive systematic review to assess the prevalence and disparities of CIAF, offering valuable insights to inform targeted nutrition interventions and policy development. Therefore, the aim of this systematic review and meta-analysis was twofold: (1) determine the pooled prevalence of CIAF and its derivatives, including the Composite Index of Severe Anthropometric Failure (CISAF) and extended CIAF (ECIAF), among children under 5 and (2) identify inequalities in the burden of CIAF.

## Methods

For this systematic review and meta-analysis, we followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines for reporting (online supplemental file 1). Using search terms developed based on the ‘Population, Concept and Context (PCC) framework’, we searched Embase/Ovid, MEDLINE (PubMed), Scopus, Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL)/EBSCO, ProQuest (EBSCO), Science Direct, Global Index Medicus, African Journals Online (AJOL) and Directory of Open Access Journals (DOAJ), Google Scholar (first 200 results) and the Cochrane Library for original articles. The reference lists of eligible articles were manually examined for additional relevant published articles. We also conducted a supplementary search in Epistemonikos, a comprehensive repository of systematic reviews. We included studies that were published between 1 January 2000 and 31 December 2024 following the introduction of the CIAF in 2000 by Svedberg, without geographic and language restrictions. We included observational studies and, where appropriate, interventional studies that provided relevant prevalence or inequality data. Qualitative studies, reviews, editorials, personal opinions, short communications, conference proceedings and case reports were excluded. The databases were first searched on 27 November 2024, and with weekly alerts and updates up to 31 December 2024, using the same criteria. The broad search terms and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) covering terms related to the CIAF, stunting, wasting, underweight, prevalence and inequalities were pretested to ensure they captured relevant records. The full search strategy and keywords for all databases are described in online supplemental file 1. The protocol for this study was registered at PROSPERO (CRD42022354416). All articles were uploaded into EndNote to identify and remove duplicates. After exporting the remaining records to Covidence, two reviewers (BS and LM) independently screened the titles, abstracts and full texts. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer (PW). The references of eligible full-text articles were searched and any additional articles identified were screened using the same eligibility criteria. In cases where multiple publications reported on the same study population using identical datasets, the most comprehensive publication was included in the review. Articles excluded after full-text assessment, along with the reasons for exclusion, are also presented in the footnote of figure 1.

A pretested (n=5 articles) Microsoft Excel spreadsheet was used to extract relevant data from each included study. The extracted data included key study details such as the first author’s name, publication year, country, study design, sample size, setting, study population and prevalence. Data were extracted by one reviewer (BS) and checked for completeness, accuracy and consistency by a second reviewer (LM). The Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) and Hoy et al’s quality assessment checklists were used for cohort and prevalence studies, respectively. The risk of bias for each study was reported; however, no study was excluded from the analysis based on quality. The quality assessment of included studies and overall risk of bias are described in detail in online supplemental file 2.

The primary outcome was the prevalence of CIAF. Studies conducted before 2006 used the National Center for Health Statistics growth references, while those conducted in 2006 or later applied the WHO (2006) growth standards. Wasting was defined as a weight-for-height z-score <−2, stunting as a height-for-age z-score <−2 and underweight as a weight-for-age z-score <−2. A descriptive summary was used to present the characteristics of the included studies. Given the substantial heterogeneity (I² ≥50%) observed, a random-effects meta-analyses model was applied to compute pooled prevalence estimates (CIAF, CISAF and eCIAF) with 95% CIs. Heterogeneity across studies was assessed using Higgins et al I² statistics. Subgroup analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of results based on factors such as country, region and geographical focus of the studies. We also incorporated the Human Development Index (HDI) to classify countries into development groups (low, medium, high and very high) and explored the socioeconomic burden of CIAF within these categories. HDI is a composite index that reflects a country’s level of human development, based on factors such as life expectancy, literacy rates, access to education and per capita gross domestic product. The Global Hunger Index (GHI) Severity Scale was also used to categorise countries into hunger severity levels (extremely alarming, alarming, serious, moderate and low) for subgroup analysis. The GHI score is calculated based on four key indicators: undernourishment, child stunting, child wasting and child mortality. We used the Gini coefficient in our meta-regression to examine the relationship between income inequality and the prevalence of the CIAF. A cumulative meta-analysis was performed by sequentially adding studies based on publication year and sample size to assess changes in pooled estimates over time. To analyse trends in CIAF prevalence over time, pooled estimates were calculated for each year and visualised using a line graph. Univariate autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) modelling was applied to annual pooled CIAF prevalence estimates from 2005 to 2024. Model identification and selection were guided by assessment of stationarity, autocorrelation structures and standard information criteria, with residual diagnostics used to confirm model adequacy. The best-fitting model was used to generate out-of-sample forecasts for 2025–2030 with 95% prediction intervals.

Data from eligible studies were extracted to assess inequalities based on sex, age, residence, maternal education and wealth quintiles. To evaluate inequalities in the prevalence of CIAF among children under 5 years old, we employed four key measures in random-effects meta-analyses: prevalence ratios (PRs), prevalence differences (PDs), Concentration Index (CIX) and Slope Index of Inequality (SII). To quantify relative disparities, PR was calculated as the ratio of CIAF prevalence between comparator groups (eg, male vs female, rural vs urban, poorest vs richest households, no maternal education vs higher education). PD was computed as the absolute difference in CIAF prevalence between these groups, providing a measure of absolute disparity. The CIX, ranging from −1 to +1, was used to evaluate relative socioeconomic inequality, with negative values indicating a disproportionate concentration of CIAF among disadvantaged groups (eg, poorer households or less-educated mothers). The SII quantified absolute socioeconomic inequality by estimating the difference in CIAF prevalence between the most and least advantaged groups across the socioeconomic spectrum, accounting for the entire distribution of wealth or education. These measures were pooled across studies to derive robust estimates of CIAF prevalence disparities by sex, geographic setting, household wealth and maternal education.

Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots and Egger’s regression. A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was applied to determine the influence of individual studies on the pooled estimates. All statistical analyses were performed using STATA (V.17; StataCorp) and R software, V.4.4.3 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

## Results

Our electronic search retrieved 6766 records, of which 3132 were duplicates. 36 articles were identified via other methods, including citation searching. We screened 3634 titles and abstracts to find 183 articles (including articles identified via citation search) that met our inclusion criteria for full-text review. After the full-text assessment, 107 articles were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis (figure 1).

A total of 107 studies from 18 different countries were included in our systematic review and meta-analysis. Of these, 103 articles were included in the CIAF meta-analysis, encompassing data from 2 651 751 under-5 children. The eCIAF meta-analysis included eight studies with a combined sample of 590 744 children, while the CISAF meta-analysis was based on four studies involving 29 905 children. Almost all the included studies were cross-sectional studies. The earliest study was conducted in 2005. The reported sample sizes included primary studies in the systematic review, ranging from 530 906 children aged 0 to 59 months to 141 among children aged 24 to 59 months. The mean age (±SD) of children in primary studies was 28.15 months (±12.02).

The general characteristics of the included studies in these systematic reviews are summarised in table 1. 67 of the primary studies included in this study were from Asia, indicating strong regional representations. Of these, 54 studies were conducted in India, 8 in Pakistan and 6 in Bangladesh. Africa contributed 17 studies, from Ethiopia (n=9), Nigeria (n=3), Ghana (n=1), Kenya (n=1), Malawi (n=1), Niger (n=1) and Tanzania (n=1). East Asia and Pacific contributed ten studies, which were from Indonesia (n=6), China (n=2), Malaysia (n=1) and Thailand (n=1). Five studies were from Latin America (four from Argentina and one from Guatemala). Europe and Middle East contributed one study each, Yemen and Turkey. Additionally, five studies were conducted across multiple countries. The eight studies included in the eCIAF meta-analysis were conducted in India, Bangladesh, Pakistan, Nigeria, Ghana, Argentina and Turkey. The four studies included in the CISAF meta-analysis comprised a multicountry study covering Bangladesh, Pakistan and Nepal, two studies from Bangladesh and one study from Ethiopia. A subgroup analysis was conducted due to high heterogeneity, based on variables such as sample size, setting, regions where the studies were conducted, study design, Human Development Groups (HDGs) and the GHI. Online supplemental file 4, table 1 presents the subgroup analysis of CIAF, CISAF and eCIAF by different factors. There is a notable geographic variation in the burden of CIAF. In the subgroup analysis, the prevalence of CIAF among children in Asia was 55.56% (95% CI 53.27% to 57.85%), in Africa it was 41.77% (95% CI 36.00% to 47.53%), in East Asia and Pacific was 37.67% (95% CI 29.32% to 46.02%), in Latin America 20.48% (95% CI 5.94% to 56.90%), in Europe 4.30% (95% CI 2.91% to 5.69%) and the highest prevalence was reported from a single study from the Middle East 70.10% (95% CI 67.60% to 72.60%). Studies with very large sample sizes (>100 000 participants) reported a relatively higher prevalence of CIAF at 53.54% (95% CI 50.13% to 56.96%). Similarly, studies with a cross-sectional design showed a prevalence of 49.35% (95% CI 46.28% to 52.43%), while studies conducted in local areas reported a prevalence of 49.53% (95% CI 45.03% to 54.03%). Moreover, the subgroup analysis of the CIAF revealed notable variations across countries classified by HDG and the GHI severity scale. Among the HDG categories, countries with high human development exhibited the lowest prevalence of anthropometric failure 31.34% (95% CI 20.00% to 42.68%). In contrast, countries with medium and low human development had the highest CIAF prevalence 54.57% (95% CI 52.28% to 56.86%) and 43.59% (95% CI 33.96% to 53.23%), respectively. When assessed by GHI severity, countries with low hunger severity score demonstrated a significantly lower CIAF prevalence of 11.73% (95% CI 4.76% to 18.70%). This figure increased substantially in countries with moderate hunger severity score, where the CIAF prevalence reached 48.17% (95% CI 39.43% to 56.91%), and in those with serious hunger severity it was 52.54% (95% CI 49.96% to 55.11%). Notably, the single country (Yemen) classified under the alarming GHI category had the highest CIAF prevalence 70.10% (95% CI 67.60% to 72.60%).

The subgroup analysis of CIAF prevalence by country revealed that the highest burden was reported in Guatemala (74.2%, 95% CI 73.43% to 74.97%) and Yemen (70.1%, 95% CI 67.60% to 72.60%), while the lowest prevalence was recorded in Turkey (4.3%, 95% CI 2.91% to 5.69%). Among Asian and African countries, Bangladesh had the highest prevalence of CIAF in Asia (61.57%, 95% CI 49.99% to 73.15%), while Malawi reported the highest prevalence in Africa (56.4%, 95% CI 55.81% to 56.99%) (figure 3). Similarly, the highest prevalence of eCIAF was reported in Asia at 49.67% (95% CI 43.23% to 56.11%), followed by Africa 41.90% (95% CI 41.21% to 42.59%). In contrast, for CISAF, comparable prevalence rates were observed between Asia and Africa, 12.40% (95% CI 9.94% to 14.85%) and 13.90% (95% CI 12.97% to 14.83%), respectively.

In the subgroup analysis by region, the prevalence of ‘wasting only’ was highest in the Middle East (10.4%), followed by Asia (5.8%) and Africa (4.6%). In contrast, ‘stunting only’ was most prevalent in Africa (23.8%), followed by Asia (14.9%) and Latin America (10.6%). The prevalence of ‘underweight only’ was also relatively higher in Africa (6.2%) compared with other regions. Regarding categories within the CIAF, the combined condition of ‘stunting and underweight’ was most prevalent in the Middle East (19.6%), followed by Asia (17.5%) and Africa (13.6%) (online supplemental file 4, tables 2 and 3).

Subgroup analysis at the country level revealed that Yemen had the highest reported prevalence of several undernutrition conditions, including ‘wasting only’ (10.4%), ‘wasting and underweight’ (15.3%), and ‘wasting, stunting and underweight’ (14.3%); however, these findings are based on a single study and should be interpreted with caution. Thailand reported the highest prevalence of ‘underweight only’ (16.8%). In Guatemala, ‘stunting only’ and ‘stunting and underweight’ were notably prevalent, at 39.2% and 32.8%, respectively (online supplemental file 4, tables 2 and 3).

Online supplemental file 4, figure 1 presents the annual pooled prevalence of CIAF from 2005 to 2024, based on studies published in each respective year, along with the distribution of CIAF subcategories over time. The estimated CIAF prevalence varied substantially across years, ranging from a high of 65.0% (95% CI 59.87% to 70.13%) in 2009 to a low of 38.2% (95% CI 30.90% to 45.50%) in 2010. The pooled prevalence decreased from 59.8% (95% CI 59.18% to 60.42%) in 2005 to 45.9% (95% CI 36.4% to 55.4%) in 2024.

Across CIAF subcategories, ‘wasting only’ exhibited variable prevalence over time, with higher estimates observed in certain years (eg, 2016 and 2024), while ‘stunting only’ consistently represented the most prevalent form of anthropometric failure. By the end of 2024, ‘stunting only’ remained the most prevalent form of undernutrition, affecting 19.09% of under-5 children. Other composite categories (eg, stunting and underweight; stunting, wasting and underweight) showed marked inter-annual variability without a consistent directional pattern (online supplemental file 4, table 4).

When grouped by global development eras, pooled CIAF prevalence estimates differed between the MDG-related publication period (2005–2015) and the Sustainable Development Goal (SDG)-related period (2016–2024). During the MDG period, reported CIAF prevalence ranged from 38.2% to 68.6% (mean: 55.3%), while during the SDG period, estimates ranged from 43.8% to 54.4% (mean: 49.2%), with Y-by-Y changes (−55.4% to +16.7%). ARIMA-based projections, derived from these pooled annual estimates, suggest a modest decline in CIAF prevalence to 42.5% by 2030 (95% CI 38.1% to 46.9%). These forecasts should be interpreted as illustrative trend extensions based on historical publication patterns and not as definitive predictions of future global CIAF prevalence (online supplemental file 4, figure 2).

A univariate meta-regression model with publication year, sample size and GHI as covariates was run to identify potential sources of heterogeneity across primary studies. In our meta-regression analysis, publication year (OR: 0.43, 95% CI 0.23 to 0.81, p=0.009) and GHI (OR: 3.55, 95% CI 2.21 to 5.72, p<0.001) were found to be statistically significant sources of heterogeneity. The finding suggests that more recent studies tend to report lower CIAF prevalence, with a significant decrease in odds by 57% per unit increase in the year of publication. Conversely, higher GHI scores are associated with significantly increased odds of CIAF prevalence. In contrast, sample size was not a significant contributor to the observed heterogeneity in the meta-regression model (OR: 1.01, 95% CI 0.99 to 1.01, p=0.693). Online supplemental file 4, figure 3a,b shows a bubble plot to visually assess trends over time and explore how year of publication and sample size might influence the observed CIAF prevalence across studies. The meta-regression examines the relationship between the Gini coefficient and CIAF, using a random-effects model. The results suggest that a higher Gini coefficient is significantly associated with increased CIAF, with a large effect size (p<0.001).

No evidence of publication bias was detected, as indicated by the symmetrical inverted funnel plots for CIAF, eCIAF and CISAF, with Egger’s test p values of 0.124, 0.674 and 0.435, respectively (online supplemental file 5, figure 1a–d). A Galbraith plot also showed that the included studies on CIAF are evenly distributed on both sides of the plot and follow a roughly linear pattern. Sensitivity analysis was also conducted to assess the stability and reliability of the pooled prevalence results for CIAF, CISAF and eCIAF, and to determine whether observed heterogeneity was driven by a few studies. Performing one leave-out study showed a pooled prevalence of CIAF ranging from 49.8% (95% CI 46.8% to 52.9%) to 50.2% (95% CI 47.3% to 53.1%). Online supplemental file 5, figure 2 displays publication bias for different CIAF categories. A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis showed that no individual study substantially influenced the pooled CIAF estimates. The leave-one-out sensitivity analysis results for CIAF, eCIAF or CISAF are presented in online supplemental file 5, figure 3a–c.

We used five equities stratifies including: sex, age, place of residence, maternal education and household wealth quintile to assess disparities in the burden of CIAF among children. Equiplot and forest plots illustrating disparities in CIAF prevalence across each dimension are provided in online supplemental file 6, figure 1–3.

Table 2B presents a summary of pooled CIAF prevalence disaggregated by these inequality measures. The pooled prevalence of CIAF was higher among male children 47.71% (95% CI 43.43% to 52.00%; n=451 175) compared with female children 46.46% (95% CI 41.98% to 50.93%; n=423 899). Online supplemental file 6, figure 2 illustrates gender disparities in CIAF prevalence stratified by country, revealing that in many countries included in the analysis, the prevalence was consistently higher among males. We also disaggregated CIAF prevalence by age group (<6 months, 6–11 months, 12–23 months, 24–35 months, 36–47 months and 48–59 months). The highest prevalence was observed among children aged 12–23 months, 49.31% (95% CI 43.28% to 55.34%; n=108 684), while the lowest was recorded among those younger than 6 months, 39.92% (95% CI 31.45% to 48.38%; n=28 881).

The prevalence of CIAF decreased with increasing levels of maternal education. Children of mothers with no formal education had the highest prevalence of CIAF 56.38% (95% CI 48.07% to 64.70%; n=144 022), followed by those whose mothers had primary education (49.24%; 95% CI 43.94% to 54.54%; n=100 717) and secondary education (44.34%; 95% CI 38.71% to 49.97%; n=255 465). The lowest prevalence was observed among children of mothers with higher education (37.43%; 95% CI 29.34% to 45.53%; n=26 647). CIAF prevalence also varied by place of residence, with higher burden observed among children in rural areas (50.98%; 95% CI 46.31% to 55.65%; n=326 037) compared with urban areas (43.50%; 95% CI 38.87% to 48.12%; n=260 963). In terms of household wealth, children in the poorest quintile had the highest prevalence (57.61%; 95% CI 44.67% to 70.56%), followed by those in poorer (51.80%; 95% CI 42.30% to 61.29%), middle (46.39%; 95% CI 39.19% to 53.59%) and richer (39.69%; 95% CI 32.46% to 46.93%) households. The lowest prevalence was reported among children in the richest households (29.36%; 95% CI 21.82% to 36.90%) (table 2B).

Online supplemental file 7, table 1 presents a meta-analysis of inequality metrics assessing disparities in the prevalence of CIAF. Measures include PR and PD, the CIX of inequality and SII capturing both relative and absolute dimensions of inequality. Online supplemental file 7 also presents the forest plots and sensitivity analysis results for each inequality metric.

A meta-analysis of 54 studies assessed the pooled PR of CIAF among under-5 children, comparing males to females. The results showed a 4% higher CIAF prevalence in males (pooled PR: 1.04, 95% CI 0.99 to 1.09); however, this difference was not statistically significant. Similarly, the pooled PD indicated no significant disparity in CIAF prevalence between male and female children (pooled PD: −1.44, 95% CI −2.91 to 0.03) (online supplemental file 7, figure 1a,b). In contrast, a meta-analysis of 19 studies comparing rural versus urban settings demonstrated an 18% higher CIAF prevalence in rural areas (pooled PR: 1.18, 95% CI 1.10 to 1.27). Correspondingly, the pooled PD confirmed a significantly elevated CIAF prevalence in rural areas compared with urban areas (pooled PD: 7.73, 95% CI 4.41 to 11.04).

Our meta-analysis revealed significant socioeconomic disparities in the prevalence of CIAF among children under 5. Children from the poorest wealth quintiles exhibited a 71% higher CIAF prevalence compared with those from the richest quintiles (pooled PR: 1.71, 95% CI 1.42 to 2.05). Similarly, children of mothers without formal education faced a 55% increased CIAF prevalence relative to those with educated mothers (pooled PR: 1.55, 95% CI 1.26 to 1.90). Pooled PD estimates further confirmed that children from poorer households and those with uneducated mothers experience a significantly higher burden of CIAF compared with their more advantaged counterparts.

To quantify socioeconomic inequalities, we employed the CIX and SII using random-effects meta-analyses. The pooled CIX for maternal education, derived from 13 studies, was −0.08 (95% CI −0.11 to −0.05), indicating a modest pro-poor inequality where CIAF is disproportionately concentrated among children of mothers with no education. Similarly, the pooled CIX for household wealth was −0.11 (95% CI −0.12 to −0.10), reflecting a statistically significant concentration of CIAF among children from poorer households.

Absolute inequalities were assessed using the SII. For wealth, the pooled SII was −31.28 (95% CI −40.43 to −22.12), indicating a substantial absolute disparity in CIAF prevalence favouring children from richer households. For maternal education, the pooled SII was −26.60 (95% CI −38.14 to −15.06), confirming a significant absolute inequality favouring children of mothers with higher education (online supplemental file 7, table 1).

## Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis is the first to comprehensively assess the global prevalence of childhood undernutrition using the CIAF, CISAF and eCIAF among children under 5 years of age, as well as to examine disparities in CIAF distribution. A total of 107 studies from 18 countries were included, the majority of which were conducted in LMICs. Our findings indicate a substantial burden of multiple anthropometric failures, affecting nearly half (49.1%) of children under 5. The pooled prevalence of CISAF and eCIAF was 12.7% and 40.4%, respectively. While the prevalence of CIAF declined from 59.8% in 2005 to 45.9% in 2024, undernutrition remains a major concern, particularly stunting, which is the most common form of anthropometric failure. Substantial geographic variation was observed in CIAF prevalence, with the highest reported in the Middle East (70.1%, based on a single study), followed by Asia (55.6%) and Africa (41.8%). Age-stratified and sex-stratified subgroup analyses revealed higher CIAF prevalence in male children and in those aged 12–23 months. Across most countries, CIAF was consistently more prevalent in males. Socioeconomic inequalities were also pronounced, with children from rural areas, the poorest households and mothers with no formal education showing significantly higher CIAF prevalence. Similarly, eCIAF showed marked regional disparities, with the highest prevalence observed in Asia (49.7%) and Africa (41.9%). In contrast, CISAF prevalence was relatively comparable between Asia and Africa (12.4% and 13.9%, respectively).

The high prevalence of CIAF identified in this study highlights the widespread nature of child undernutrition but also exposes persistent structural and social inequalities. While no formal cut-off exists for CIAF, the observed prevalence far surpasses the WHO criteria for individual indicators-wasting ≥15% and stunting ≥30%, which are classified as ‘very high’ and warrant urgent public health intervention. The World Health Assembly’s Global Nutrition Targets aim to reduce stunting by 50% and maintain wasting below 3% by 2030; however, most LMICs are not on track to meet these goals, with projections extending to 2050 for many. Importantly, the high burden of multiple anthropometric failures underscores the limitations of conventional single-indicator approaches (eg, stunting, wasting and underweight only) and supports the adoption of composite indices in tracking nutrition targets and surveillance. Addressing this complex burden requires intensified, integrated global efforts and equity-focused interventions that target both immediate nutritional needs and the broader social determinants of health.

Our analysis further indicates that CIAF prevalence declined from 59.8% in 2005 to 45.9% in 2024, with an average annual reduction of 0.73%. Despite this modest progress, the current trajectory suggests that achieving the SDGs related to undernutrition remains unlikely. In support of this, the 2023 Joint Child Malnutrition Estimates reported that despite a one-third reduction in stunting prevalence over the past decade, progress has been uneven, hindered by the persistent triple burden of malnutrition. As the double and triple burden of malnutrition continues to rise globally, the use of comprehensive metrics is increasingly essential for tracking progress and identifying gaps. In this context, the CIAF offers a more inclusive indicator that captures the co-occurrence of multiple forms of anthropometric failure, providing a clearer picture of the true extent of childhood undernutrition. We argue that incorporating a measure such as the CIAF into national and international childhood undernutrition metrics is essential for accurately identifying the current burden and epidemiological distribution of undernutrition, and for better capturing the true extent of nutritional deficits among children. To our knowledge, no prior global systematic analysis has employed the CIAF to monitor progress toward SDG targets. Given that undernutrition rarely occurs in isolation, understanding the patterns and trends of overlapping anthropometric failures is critical for informing effective policy responses, particularly in LMICs. Monitoring CIAF can help identify high-burden countries and guide the development of context-specific, equity-focused strategies to reduce undernutrition among vulnerable populations.

Our meta-analysis confirms that the burden of severe anthropometric failure remains alarmingly high, with a pooled CISAF prevalence of 12.7%. This burden is particularly concentrated in Africa (13.9%) and Asia (12.4%), regions that together account for over 97% of children with severe acute malnutrition (SAM). These findings highlight the urgent need for intensified nutrition interventions in these high-burden regions, given the well-established link between severe undernutrition and under-5 mortality. Although no studies to date have directly linked the CISAF to child mortality in these regions, there is ample evidence that SAM significantly increases the risk of death. SAM is known to significantly increase the risk of death by weakening immune defences, raising susceptibility to infections such as pneumonia and diarrhoea, and causing metabolic disturbances and clinical complications like bilateral pitting oedema. These pathophysiological mechanisms help explain the high fatality rates observed among children with multiple anthropometric deficits. Moreover, existing evidence indicates that children facing multiple forms of malnutrition are at a significantly greater risk of mortality compared with those affected by just one form of anthropometric failure. The continued high prevalence of CISAF in these regions thus highlights a critical health challenge and an opportunity for targeted, life-saving interventions.

The eCIAF is a valuable metric for capturing the coexistence of undernutrition and overnutrition in children, reflecting the full spectrum of malnutrition. Despite the limited number of available studies, our meta-analysis found that approximately 40.4% of children under 5 were affected by eCIAF, highlighting a growing and under-recognised burden. This high prevalence likely reflects the global rise in childhood overweight and obesity, according to recent reports, which have not declined in any region of the world over the past decade and have increased in Asia, Africa and Latin America. These trends underscore the persistent and overlapping challenges of undernutrition and overnutrition, particularly in LMICs. As the global nutrition landscape shifts, it is imperative to adopt integrated strategies that concurrently address both forms of malnutrition.

Our subgroup meta-analysis by geographical region identified Yemen as an outlier with an exceptionally high reported CIAF prevalence. However, this estimate (70.1%) is derived from a single included study and should therefore be interpreted as study-specific rather than as a nationally consolidated estimate supported by multiple independent data sources. While caution is warranted in generalising this finding, it is broadly consistent with external evidence indicating a severe burden of child undernutrition in Yemen. For example, the GHI ranks Yemen 126th out of 127 countries, classifying its situation as ‘alarming,’ with reported prevalences of 48.5% stunting, 16.8% wasting and 39.5% overall undernourishment. Similarly, a regional review of child nutritional status reported high levels of undernutrition in Yemen, including 46.4% stunting, 39.9% underweight and 16.4% wasting. The markedly high CIAF prevalence observed in our analysis likely reflects the compounded effects of prolonged armed conflict, widespread food insecurity, limited access to health services and persistent socioeconomic instability. Together, these intersecting vulnerabilities may contribute to the coexistence of multiple anthropometric failures among Yemeni children, explaining Yemen’s outlier status in the subgroup analysis while underscoring the need for additional nationally representative studies.

Asia and sub-Saharan Africa bear the greatest burden of CIAF, with pooled CIAF prevalences of 55.5% and 41.7%, respectively. These findings are consistent with global estimates showing that 52% of all stunted children live in Asia and 43% in Africa, highlighting the enduring challenge of chronic malnutrition across both regions. Southern Asia, in particular, has the highest global prevalence of wasting (14.3%) and accounts for over three-quarters of all children affected by severe wasting. The convergence of these forms of undernutrition contributes to the high CIAF burden observed in the region. In contrast, East Asia and the Pacific and Latin America reported relatively lower CIAF prevalence, 37.7% and 23.7%, respectively. Although Latin America has achieved progress in reducing child undernutrition over the past two decades, recent evidence suggests that these gains are slowing improvement. These patterns emphasise the need for sustained and accelerated action to address the overlapping forms of undernutrition, particularly in regions where multiple anthropometric failures remain widespread.

Our subgroup analysis shows a strong association between economic development and undernutrition, with CIAF prevalence significantly higher in countries with medium (54.6%) or low (43.6%) HDI levels, compared with 31.3% in countries with high HDI. A similar pattern emerged with respect to the GHI: CIAF prevalence increased from 9.4% in countries with low hunger to 48.2% in those with moderate, 52.6% in those with serious and 70.1% in those with alarming GHI categories. Although global hunger has declined over recent decades, the growing prevalence of the double and triple burden of malnutrition and multiple nutritional failures poses new and complex challenges in LMICs. Emerging evidence indicates an upward trend in the co-occurrence of multiple forms of malnutrition in LMICs, complicating future efforts. Addressing this complex challenge requires coordinated, multisectoral action at both global and national levels.

Socioeconomic and demographic inequalities are key determinants of child undernutrition worldwide, and our findings reveal clear disparities in the prevalence of CIAF across multiple population subgroups. Children from lower socioeconomic backgrounds, rural areas and households with limited maternal education consistently experienced higher rates of multiple anthropometric failures. Notably, CIAF prevalence was slightly higher among males than females (47.7% vs 46.5%), aligning with evidence from large population-based studies and a systematic review reporting higher rates of undernutrition among boys. Age-specific disparities showed the highest CIAF prevalence among children aged 12–23 months (49.3%), likely reflecting the critical window of nutritional vulnerability during the complementary feeding period, when poor dietary diversity and heightened exposure to infections converge to increase nutritional risk.

Our findings consistently highlight that relative and absolute inequalities in CIAF prevalence are pronounced across wealth and education gradients, with the burden disproportionately affecting the poorest and least educated groups. CIAF prevalence was 56.4% among children whose mothers had no formal education, compared with 37.4% among those whose mothers had higher education. This disparity highlights the role of maternal knowledge, health literacy, caregiving practices and access to resources in shaping child nutrition outcomes. A similarly pronounced wealth gradient was observed, with CIAF prevalence being highest among children from the poorest households (57.6%) and lowest among those from the wealthiest (29.4%). These findings are consistent with extensive evidence documenting the impact of poverty on child nutrition through pathways such as food insecurity, limited healthcare access and poor living conditions. Together, these patterns underscore the urgent need for equity-focused interventions that address the structural determinants of child undernutrition.

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis to synthesise global evidence on childhood undernutrition using the CIAF. The study provides a comprehensive analysis of the CIAF as an alternative indicator for assessing childhood nutrition status. The findings demonstrate that CIAF captures a broader spectrum of nutritional deficits than conventional indicators such as stunting, wasting and underweight, offering valuable insight into aggregated nutritional failures. This study’s expanded scope indicates the potential of CIAF to inform more targeted and effective public health interventions. Additionally, using inequality measurements further strengthens our understanding of CIAF and enhances the relevance of our findings to regions where undernutrition is endemic. This study has limitations. The evidence included in this review is geographically imbalanced, with a heavy concentration of studies from South Asia, particularly India and Pakistan, and relatively limited representation from Europe and the Middle East. As a result, the pooled prevalence estimates largely reflect the burden of CIAF in LMICs, especially in South Asia and Africa. While this aligns with regions bearing the highest burden of child undernutrition, the findings may not be fully generalisable to underrepresented regions with different epidemiological, socioeconomic and health system contexts. Additionally, we observed substantial heterogeneity between the studies included in the meta-analysis, which is expected when pooling data from diverse populations, geographic regions, study designs and survey years. Such pronounced between-study variability likely reflects contextual differences in the burden of CIAF rather than methodological inconsistency alone. Consequently, while the pooled prevalence provides a useful summary estimate, it should be interpreted with caution, and greater emphasis should be placed on the range of observed estimates and subgroup-specific findings. As this study used an univariate approach that relies solely on historical trends and does not incorporate external determinants of undernutrition, forecasts should be interpreted cautiously. Our meta-analysis of CISAF was based on four studies that may have been underpowered.

Trend analyses of CIAF prevalence based on the published literature suggest differences in the distribution of pooled estimates between the MDG-related (2005–2015) and SDG-related (2016–2024) periods; however, these patterns should be interpreted with caution. Apparent volatility during the MDG era and comparatively narrower ranges during the SDG period likely reflect changes in the geographic composition, population coverage and sample sizes of studies published in each period rather than true temporal shifts in global CIAF burden. Consequently, these findings should not be construed as evidence of causal progress attributable to the SDGs. Instead, they underscore the persistent high burden of anthropometric failure among children under 5, particularly in LMICs and highlight the continued need for accelerated, equitable and context-specific interventions to advance progress toward SDG 2 (Zero Hunger). Despite these limitations, our findings offer important insights into the burden of multiple anthropometric failures among children under 5, particularly in low-income contexts. These findings can support resource prioritisation, strategic planning and policy development to reduce childhood malnutrition, particularly in low-income settings.

## Conclusions

The CIAF affected nearly half of children under 5, with substantial regional, socioeconomic and demographic disparities. This meta-analysis reveals that CIAF disproportionately affects children in rural areas compared with urban settings, with no significant sex-based differences, while socioeconomic disparities are pronounced, as prevalence is markedly higher in the poorest households and among children of uneducated mothers, with pro-poor inequalities and substantial absolute disparities. Despite modest declines in CIAF over the past two decades, the current rate of progress is insufficient to meet global nutrition targets. Among the subcategories of CIAF, stunting remains the dominant form of undernutrition. Our findings highlight the need for equity-driven, multisectoral nutritional interventions, particularly in LMICs, to address these complex and overlapping forms of undernutrition. Embracing comprehensive measures, such as double-duty actions, will be essential to achieving global nutrition targets and reducing child undernutrition in all its forms.

To effectively address the global burden of child undernutrition, especially in low-resource settings, health metrics should integrate composite indices such as CIAF, eCIAF and CISAF into national and international monitoring systems, as these indicators better reflect the coexistence of multiple anthropometric deficits. Incorporating CIAF into national and global nutrition surveillance frameworks is crucial to support progress toward SDG targets and improve child health outcomes. The SDG period shows more consistent reductions in CIAF prevalence than the MDGs’ erratic trends. However, the persistent high prevalence of CIAF indicates that SDG nutritional targets remain challenging, requiring intensified, targeted interventions to accelerate progress. Furthermore, addressing significant disparities in undernutrition requires equity-focused interventions that prioritise the needs of the most vulnerable children, including those living in poverty, in rural areas and mothers without formal education. Given the increasing overlap of undernutrition and overnutrition, policy responses should incorporate double-duty actions that address both forms simultaneously through improvements in food systems, maternal education and early childhood care. Additionally, countries experiencing conflict, food insecurity and economic fragility, such as Yemen, require urgent, context-specific nutrition interventions and sustained humanitarian support to prevent further deterioration in child nutritional outcomes.
