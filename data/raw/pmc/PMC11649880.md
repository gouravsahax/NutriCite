# Recombinant erythropoietin for the treatment of iron deficiency anemia in pregnancy: A systematic review

PMCID: PMC11649880 | PMID: 39087437 | Journal: International journal of gynaecology and obstetrics: the official organ of the International Federation of Gynaecology and Obstetrics (2025)

## Abstract

AbstractBackgroundTreatment options for severe, refractory iron deficiency anemia are limited in pregnancy.ObjectiveTo review the available literature on the use of recombinant erythropoietin in the treatment of iron deficiency anemia in pregnancy.Search StrategyAn electronic search of seven databases from inception to March 2022 was performed using a combination of keywords.Selection CriteriaWe included all randomized controlled or observational studies of pregnant patients with iron deficiency anemia who received recombinant erythropoietin or control. The primary outcome was a change in hematologic parameters (hemoglobin or hematocrit) after treatment. Studies were appraised using the criteria outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions.Data Collection and AnalysisData were summarized using narrative synthesis and descriptive statistics as appropriate. This study was registered with PROSPERO, CRD42022313328.Main ResultsOf 234 studies screened, five studies met the inclusion criteria and had sufficient data for analysis (n = 103 recombinant erythropoietin and n = 104 controls). All patients in the intervention group received iron supplementation (intravenous or oral) in addition to recombinant erythropoietin. All patients in the control group received iron supplementation (intravenous or oral) alone. As the result of variance between studies in inclusion criteria, the timing of repeat blood draws, and data reporting, a meta‐analysis could not be performed. Three studies found that serial recombinant erythropoietin combined with iron supplementation was more effective at raising hematologic laboratory parameters (hemoglobin or hematocrit) than iron alone. One study reported no difference in hemoglobin or hematocrit levels between groups at day 28. However, patients in this study only received one dose of recombinant erythropoietin, whereas those in the other studies received serial doses. Another study also found no difference in hemoglobin levels by day 28, but patients in the recombinant erythropoietin group had lower hemoglobin levels at baseline and a more rapid rise in hemoglobin than iron alone. This is demonstrated by a more significant rise in hemoglobin at day 11 in the recombinant erythropoietin group than in the control group.ConclusionsSerial recombinant erythropoietin administration and iron supplementation may be more effective at treating refractory iron deficiency anemia in pregnancy than iron supplementation alone.

## INTRODUCTION

Anemia is a common hematologic disorder affecting up to one‐third of pregnant women in the USA. Anemia is categorized by etiology (i.e., acquired or inherited), mechanism (i.e. decreased red blood cell production or increased red blood cell destruction), and red blood cell size (i.e. microcytic, macrocytic, or normocytic). The most common cause of anemia in pregnancy is iron deficiency anemia (IDA), an acquired, microcytic anemia caused by decreased red blood cell production. IDA is associated with poor maternal and fetal outcomes, such as an increased risk of pre‐eclampsia, postpartum hemorrhage, cesarean delivery, infection, and postpartum depression, as well as low birth weight, preterm birth, fetal growth restriction, and altered fetal neurodevelopment., ,

First‐line treatment for IDA is oral or intravenous iron supplementation. Treatment of severe or refractory IDA in pregnancy is limited to blood transfusions. There are several drawbacks and limitations of blood transfusions to treat anemia, such as the risk of infection, patient refusal for religious or cultural beliefs, and national blood shortages. Therefore, alternative therapies for treating severe or refractory IDA in pregnancy are needed.

Endogenous erythropoietin (EPO), the primary regulator in the production of red blood cells, is a glycoprotein hormone produced by the kidneys in response to hypoxia and anemia. There is a physiologic rise in EPO levels during pregnancy, which increases red blood cell production. Recombinant EPO (rEPO) is a synthetic version of this hormone derived from Chinese hamster ovarian cells. Historically, rEPO use in pregnancy has been limited to pregnant patients with anemia secondary to end‐stage renal disease. Few studies have evaluated its use outside this context, such as for treating refractory IDA in pregnancy.

The objective of this study was to review the available literature on the use of rEPO in the treatment of IDA in pregnancy.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

This systematic review was performed according to the Preferred Reporting Item for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) Statement. Before data collection, the research protocol was established and the review was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (CRD42022313328).

Eligibility criteria included randomized controlled trials or observational studies that evaluated the use of rEPO in pregnant patients with IDA. Non‐controlled studies, case reports or case series, and review papers were excluded.

One author (AL), with the aid of a trained medical librarian, performed an electronic search in PubMed, Ovid Medline, Scopus, CINAHL, EMBASE, Clinicaltrials.gov, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials from inception to March 2022.

Our search strategy included a combination of keywords including, or related to, “erythropoietin”, “iron deficiency anemia”, and “pregnancy” (Appendix A). No restrictions for language or geographical location were used. The reference lists of all reviewed articles were evaluated for additional studies meeting inclusion criteria. The entire database search query is included in Appendix A.

Two authors (AL and SW) independently screened all abstracts. Studies deemed relevant were then full‐text reviewed. Any reviewer disagreements were resolved with a discussion involving a third author (HA‐K).

The risk of bias in each included study was assessed using the criteria suggested in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions. Specifically, the revised Cochrane risk‐of‐bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2.0) and the Risk of Bias in Non‐randomized studies—of Interventions (ROBINS‐I) tools were used for randomized and non‐randomized studies, respectively. The RoB 2.0 guideline uses five domains relating to the risk of bias: (1) randomization, (2) deviation from intended intervention, (3) incomplete outcome data, (4) outcome measurement, and (5) selective reporting. The ROBINS‐I guideline uses seven domains related to the risk of bias: (1) confounding bias, (2) selection bias, (3) classification of interventions, (4) deviation from intended intervention, (5) incomplete outcome data, (6) outcome measurement, and (7) selective reporting. Two reviewers (AL and SW) individually assessed the risk of bias and, for each domain, categorized the study as having a “low”, “high”, or “unclear” risk of bias. The overall risk of bias for each included study was deemed “low”, “moderate”, or “high”.

Data were independently extracted from the included studies by three authors (AL, SW, and KR). The following data were recorded without modification: study characteristics (authors, year of publication, location, sample size, inclusion and exclusion criteria, and study end point); treatment and control group medication details; gestational age at treatment initiation; baseline hemoglobin and hematocrit levels; timing of repeat laboratory analysis; hemoglobin or hematocrit levels after treatment. When information was missing from an included study, the corresponding author was contacted directly to request the non‐published data. The primary outcome was a change in hematologic parameters (hemoglobin and/or hematocrit) after treatment.

Individual study data were collected and summarized using Microsoft Excel. Narrative synthesis and descriptive statistics were used to summarize study details, baseline data, and outcomes. Continuous data from the individual studies were totaled using the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews algorithm. Within the pooled data, baseline differences between groups were assessed using a t‐test and a P‐value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Planned meta‐analysis would be performed for the primary outcome to estimate pooled mean difference with associated 95% confidence interval. However, because of variance between studies in design and data reporting, meta‐analysis could not be performed.

Three studies, , found that serial rEPO combined with iron supplementation was more effective at raising hematologic laboratory parameters (hemoglobin or hematocrit) than iron alone. Sanchez‐Gonzalez et al. and Estemberg et al. noted a significant increase in hemoglobin after treatment in the intervention group (rEPO + iron) compared with the control group (iron alone). In the study by Breymann et al., patients in the intervention group had a more significant change in hematocrit at day 28 than those in the control group (8.3% ± 1.0% versus 6.0% ± 0.7%; P = 0.003). Additionally, in this study, patients in the intervention group reached the target hemoglobin level faster (mean duration of therapy, 18 versus 25 days). Only one patient in the intervention group did not reach target hemoglobin levels after 4 weeks compared with five patients in the control group.

Findings by Sanchez‐Gonzalez et al. suggest that the effect of rEPO supplementation persists even after discontinuation. In this study, treatment was stopped when the target hemoglobin level (12.0 g/dL) was reached or after a maximum of 4 weeks. At the time of delivery, patients in the intervention group (rEPO + iron) still had a higher mean increase in hemoglobin from baseline compared with those in the control group (1.9 g/dL versus 1.2 g/dL; P = 0.013).

Krafft et al. reported no difference in hemoglobin levels by day 28. However, in this study, treatment group assignment was based on baseline hemoglobin levels. Those with lower baseline hemoglobin levels were assigned to the intervention group (rEPO + iron supplementation). In contrast, those with a higher baseline hemoglobin level were assigned to the control group (iron supplementation alone). Though the authors of this study found no difference between groups in hemoglobin levels at day 28, patients in the intervention group had a more rapid rise in hemoglobin, with a more significant increase at day 11 compared with the control group (Table 3). Similarly, Oropeza et al. reported no difference in hemoglobin or hematocrit levels between groups at day 28. However, patients in this study only received one dose of rEPO, whereas those in the four other studies received serial doses.

None of the studies reported adverse events related to rEPO or iron supplementation.

## DISCUSSION

Our review found evidence to support that rEPO combined with iron supplementation may be more effective at raising hemoglobin and/or hematocrit than iron alone. One study reported no difference in hemoglobin or hematocrit levels between groups at day 28, but patients in this study received only one dose of rEPO; conversely, in all other studies, serial doses were administered. Lastly, administration of rEPO does not appear to increase the risk of maternal or fetal adverse events.

Our findings are in line with literature that has suggested that rEPO is an effective intervention to raise hematologic parameters. Additionally, previous studies have shown that rEPO does not cross the placenta and its use is not associated with adverse fetal effects. Although we cannot definitively conclude that there is no increased risk of uncommon adverse events with the use of rEPO in pregnancy, our systematic review supports the finding that rEPO appears to be a safe treatment for use in the pregnant population.

Much of the existing literature on the use of rEPO in pregnancy is in patients with end‐stage renal disease. One of these studies suggested that compared with intravenous administration, subcutaneous administration of rEPO requires a lower dose and is therefore the preferred approach. Due to the limitations in available data, our study was unable to explore the role of route of administration on effect of the medication.

The main strength of this study is its exhaustive review and detailed data extraction. It summarizes the available literature and highlights the utility of this intervention to treat a condition with otherwise limited options. A limitation of our study is the relatively low number of participants and studies. In addition, a significant limitation of our study was the inability to perform a meta‐analysis. This was a result of variance in study design but was also due to incomplete data reporting. The corresponding authors of the individual studies were contacted to obtain the missing information to pool the data and summarize the outcomes, but they did not respond.

In conclusion, when IDA is refractory to iron supplementation, management options are limited. Our study suggests that serial rEPO administration when combined with iron supplementation, may be a safe and effective treatment option for resistant IDA. Randomized controlled trials are needed to establish level A evidence for the use of rEPO in pregnancy.
